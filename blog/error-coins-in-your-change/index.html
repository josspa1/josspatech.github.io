Most coins in change are common. A few leave the Mint with a wrong die, a wrong planchet, or a misaligned strike. This article names the error types that still turn up in pocket change, shows the hand checks collectors use, and marks the point where looking the piece up by hand becomes a stack of tabs and notes.

What counts as an error coin (and what does not)

An error coin is a piece that left the United States Mint with a minting fault: a doubled die, an off-center strike, a broadstrike, a clipped planchet, or a planchet of the wrong metal or denomination. Damage after the coin left the Mint — scratches, acid etching, jewelry holes, or machine marks — is not an error. Collectors price mint errors; they discount or ignore post-mint damage.

Common error types in circulating change

  • Doubled die — design elements appear doubled because the die was hubbed twice out of register. Famous examples include certain Lincoln cent dates; most doubling you see under a loupe is machine doubling, which is not the same variety.
  • Off-center — the blank was not centered under the dies, so part of the design is missing and blank planchet shows.
  • Broadstrike — struck without a collar, so the coin spreads wider than normal diameter and often lacks a full edge reed.
  • Clipped planchet — the blank was punched overlapping a previous punch or the strip edge, leaving a curved or straight missing section.
  • Wrong planchet / wrong metal — a blank meant for another denomination or alloy was fed into the press (for example, a cent struck on a dime planchet).

Damage that fools people

Corrosion, plating loss, heat, and intentional alteration can look dramatic and still be worth face value. Weight and diameter that match the published specs for that denomination, plus a clean strike under magnification, separate a candidate from a damaged common coin.

How to check a suspect coin by hand

Pull anything that looks wrong into a separate pile. Work one denomination at a time so diameter and weight stay comparable.

Measurements first

Weigh the coin on a scale that reads to 0.01 g and compare to the Mint specification for that denomination and era. Measure diameter with calipers. A broadstrike or wrong-planchet piece often fails one of those checks before you open a catalog.

Edge and strike

Look at the edge under light: missing reeds can signal a broadstrike; a partial design with blank field can signal off-center. Under a loupe or USB microscope, check whether doubling is raised and rounded (die doubling) or flat and shelf-like (often machine doubling).

Match a published variety

Published price guides and variety attributions (for example, listings maintained by services such as PCGS and major variety references) are how you turn “looks odd” into a named error with a market range. That match is the tedious step: you flip between photos, date/mint, and variety notes until one page fits — or none do.

Where the manual stack breaks down

One odd cent is a five-minute loupe check. A handful of candidates means repeating weight, diameter, magnification, and guide lookup for each piece, then writing down what you found so you do not re-research the same coin next month. Photos in a camera roll without a linked record drift away from the coin in the tube. Value estimates without a date and source become guesses you cannot defend to yourself later.

FAQ

Are error coins still found in pocket change?

Yes, though most odd coins are damage. Off-centers, clipped planchets, and occasional doubled dies or wrong-planchet strikes still leave circulation; frequency is low enough that a disciplined check beats hoping every shiny mark is valuable.

Does machine doubling make a coin an error?

No. Machine doubling is a strike effect that collectors treat separately from true doubled-die varieties. Under magnification, machine doubling often looks flat or shelf-like rather than raised and rounded.

Should I clean a suspect error before photographing it?

No. Cleaning can leave hairlines and lower collector interest. Photograph as found; note dirt or residue in the record instead of polishing.

What if Identify is unsure?

Treat a low-confidence result as a cue to re-shoot under even light, check weight and diameter again, or ask a dealer. The vault still stores your photos and notes so the next pass starts from the same evidence.

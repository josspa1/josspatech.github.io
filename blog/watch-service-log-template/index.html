What is a watch service log, and why does it matter?

A watch service log is a dated ledger of maintenance: the date, who did the work, what was done, cost when you know it, and when the next service should happen. It is the difference between "I think it was cleaned in 2019" and a record a buyer, insurer, or future you can use.

Provenance is part of the value

For many mechanical pieces, a clear service history supports what you paid and what you can ask later. Two identical references can sell differently when one has dated watchmaker notes and the other has an empty box.

Receipts in the box are not a log

Factory papers and shop tickets are evidence, but they are not searchable in one place. A log pulls those events into one table — or one phone history — you can sort by date or by piece.

Copy-paste spreadsheet template (free)

Paste this header row into Sheets, Excel, or Numbers — one header per cell — then freeze row 1:

Brand | Model / reference | Case serial | Caliber | Service date | Watchmaker / shop | Work performed | Cost | Next service due | Notes

Rules that keep the sheet honest:

  • One row per service event, not one row per watch. A piece serviced three times gets three rows that share brand, reference, and serial.
  • Use YYYY-MM-DD for dates so sorting never breaks.
  • Put photo filenames in Notes (`SERIAL-caseback.jpg`) if you keep images beside the sheet.
  • Optional columns you may add later: daily rate after service, amplitude, water-test result. Useful when the shop provides them; not required for a working log.

Keep the file on your own computer or phone and export a backup after every update.

Why spreadsheets fail most collectors within a year

The log is not where the watch is

Service happens at a shop counter or kitchen table. If the log lives only on a desktop file you open twice a year, you forget to update it when the piece comes home.

Photos drift away from the row

A sheet cannot sit next to a caseback photo the way a museum entry can. People end up with unlabeled camera-roll shots and a table that cannot prove which image belongs to which piece.

No nudge for the next service

Mechanical watches and many clocks want attention on an interval. A static sheet will not remind you that the five-year mark is coming.

When should I move from a spreadsheet to the phone log?

Use these triggers. Any one is survivable. Two or more together mean the spreadsheet is losing.

  • You want photos with the piece and the camera-roll folder is already a mess.
  • You edit the sheet on a laptop but stand at the shop with only your phone.
  • You own more than a handful of pieces and cannot remember which serial had the last overhaul.
  • You want a reminder before the next service window, not after the warranty ends.
  • You have started saving `service-log-FINAL-v3.xlsx` because you no longer trust one file.

Where a phone app still falls short versus a shared spreadsheet: two people editing the same live file from different computers. If that matters more than privacy on your own device, keep the sheet and a disciplined backup routine.

FAQ

What should I write under "work performed" on the spreadsheet?

Keep it short: "full overhaul", "battery", "crystal + gasket". In HHH, pick the closest service type and put extra detail in Notes.

Do I need amplitude and rate for every service?

No. Record them when the shop provides them. In HHH, use Notes; on a spreadsheet, add optional columns only if you will fill them.

Can I use this for clocks as well as watches?

Yes. My Museum holds clocks as well as watches; Service Tracker logs against those pieces. Clock oil intervals can also drive reminders when you use the clock metadata / oil tools in the app.

Is the spreadsheet template enough by itself?

For one or two pieces and rare services, yes. When you want the history in your pocket with photos on the piece and a next-due reminder, use Repairs & Service in HHH.

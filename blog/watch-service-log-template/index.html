A serious watch or clock needs a service history the next owner can trust. This article gives you a copy-paste watch service log template, explains why most spreadsheets get abandoned within a year, and shows when a phone log next to the piece is the better habit. No bank login, no account required for the template itself — the file stays on your device.

What is a watch service log, and why does it matter?

A watch service log is a dated ledger of maintenance: the date, who did the work, what was done, and how the piece performed afterward. It is the difference between "I think it was cleaned in 2019" and a record a buyer, insurer, or future you can actually use.

Provenance is part of the value

For many mechanical pieces, a clean, unbroken service history supports what you paid and what you can ask later. Two identical references can sell differently when one has dated watchmaker notes and the other has an empty box. The log is not paperwork for its own sake — it is proof the piece was cared for.

Receipts in the box are not a log

Factory papers, a handwritten shop ticket, and a credit-card slip are evidence, but they are not searchable, not dated in one place, and easy to lose when you move house. A log pulls those events into one table you can sort by date or by piece.

What columns belong in a watch service log template?

Twelve columns cover identity, the service event, post-service performance, and money. Keep every column defined the same way so the sheet stays sortable years later.

The core columns, defined

ColumnWhat goes in it
BrandName on the dial or movement, e.g. "Omega"
Model / referenceReference number if you have it; otherwise the model name
Case serialCase serial as stamped; "none" if blank or covered
CaliberMovement caliber number when known
Service dateDate the work was completed (YYYY-MM-DD)
Watchmaker / shopName or shop; city helps later
Work performedShort list: "full overhaul", "mainspring", "gasket", "regulate"
Daily rate after (s/day)Timed rate after service, seconds per day
Amplitude afterAmplitude reading if the shop gave one
Water testPressure result if tested, e.g. "3 bar pass" or "not tested"
CostWhat you paid, in your currency
NotesWarranty end date, parts replaced, photo filenames, value at log

Optional fields worth adding later

Add purchase date, box-and-papers status, or insurance value only when you need them. Empty columns invite abandoned sheets. Start with the twelve above.

Copy-paste watch service log template

Paste this header row into row 1 of Sheets, Excel, or Numbers — one header per cell — then freeze row 1:

Brand | Model / reference | Case serial | Caliber | Service date | Watchmaker / shop | Work performed | Daily rate after (s/day) | Amplitude after | Water test | Cost | Notes

Rules that keep the sheet honest:

  • One row per service event, not one row per watch. A piece serviced three times gets three rows that share the same brand, reference, and serial.
  • Use YYYY-MM-DD for dates so sorting never breaks.
  • Put photo filenames in Notes (`SERIAL-caseback.jpg`) so images stay matched to the event.
  • If the shop did not give rate or amplitude, write "not timed" — blank cells look like forgotten work.

Keep the file on your own computer or phone and export a backup after every update. Local records mean no account sits between you and the history.

Why spreadsheets fail most collectors within a year

Most enthusiasts start a sheet, fill three rows, and stop. The failure is structural, not moral.

Zero visual syncing

A spreadsheet is a wall of text. It cannot elegantly hold a macro photo of the caseback serial, the movement stamp, or the dial next to the row you just typed. You end up with a folder of unlabeled phone photos and a sheet that cannot prove which image belongs to which service.

The log is not where the watch is

You do not wear a laptop on your wrist. Service happens at a shop counter, a kitchen table, or a bench. If the log lives only on a desktop file you open twice a year, you forget to update it when the piece comes home.

No nudge for the next service

Mechanical watches and many clocks want attention on an interval. A static sheet will not remind you that the five-year mark is coming. People remember the invoice and forget the schedule.

When should I move from a spreadsheet to a phone log?

Use these triggers. Any one is survivable. Two or more together mean the spreadsheet is losing.

  • You want a photo attached to every service event and the image folder is already a mess.
  • You edit the sheet on a laptop but stand at the shop with only your phone.
  • You own more than a handful of pieces and cannot remember which serial had the last overhaul.
  • You have started saving `service-log-FINAL-v3.xlsx` because you no longer trust one file.
  • You want a reminder before the next service window, not after the warranty ends.

Where a phone app still falls short versus a shared spreadsheet: two people editing the same live file from different computers. If that matters more than privacy on your own device, keep the sheet and a disciplined backup routine.

FAQ

What should I write under "work performed"?

Keep it short and repeatable: "full overhaul", "regulate only", "crystal + gasket", "mainspring replaced". Avoid novels. Details that matter once — part numbers, warranty text — go in Notes.

Do I need amplitude and rate for every service?

No. Record them when the shop provides them. If they did not time the piece, write "not timed" so a blank cell is never mistaken for a perfect zero.

Is a digital log enough for insurance?

Insurers vary. Many want clear photos, purchase proof, and a valuation dated close to the claim. A service log supports provenance; it does not automatically replace whatever schedule your policy asks for. Ask your insurer what they require, then store those items with the same piece ID you use in the log.

Can I use this template for clocks as well as watches?

Yes. Use Brand, Model / reference, and a movement or serial field the same way. Put case type (mantel, wall, grandfather) in Notes if you do not add a separate column.

How do I inventory my coin collection for the first time?

Do a rough pass first: gather every coin into one place, sort by denomination, and record one row per coin with whatever you know now. A first catalog with gaps beats a perfect one you never finish. You can fill in mint marks and grades later, one session at a time.

Sort before you type

Pile everything by denomination: pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters, halves, dollars. Sorting physically is faster than deciding denomination in your head for each coin, and it surfaces duplicates and oddities you would otherwise miss. Work through one pile per sitting rather than the whole box.

One row per coin, not per type

Give each coin its own row, even if ten look identical. One row per coin lets you track storage location, condition, and sale of individual pieces later. If you merge duplicates into a "type" row with a quantity, you lose the ability to answer "which one is the 1969-S?"

Keep the file on your own computer or phone and export a backup regularly. Local records mean no account and no server can lose them. The same local-first idea applies to budgeting data you keep on your own phone: the file is yours.

What columns should a coin collection inventory spreadsheet include?

Nine columns cover almost everything a collector needs to track. The trick is defining each one precisely so the sheet stays sortable years later.

The core columns, defined

ColumnWhat goes in it
DenominationCoin type, e.g. "Quarter" or "Morgan Dollar"
YearDate on the coin, four digits
Mint markLetter(s) or "none"
GradeYour assessment, e.g. "AU-50" or "circulated"
Purchase priceWhat you paid, in dollars
Current value estimateYour best guess, dated in Notes
Storage locationSpecific: "safe, tray 2, row 3"
NotesVariety, errors, provenance, date of value estimate
Photo referenceFilename of the coin's photo

Grade deserves a note: unless you have paid for grading, record it as your own opinion. That keeps the sheet honest and comparable.

Optional columns worth adding later

Once the core nine are filled, collectors commonly add acquisition date, seller or source, serial or certificate number, and insurance value. Add these only when a real need appears. Empty columns invite abandoned spreadsheets.

Copy-paste template setup

Paste the nine headers into row 1 of any spreadsheet app, one per cell, then freeze row 1 so headers stay visible while scrolling. Keep one row per coin, not per type, so quantities and locations stay accurate. A spreadsheet like this stays entirely on your own device, and the same principle carries into our offline inventory app, which keeps records on your phone with no account.

Is free coin collection inventory software actually free, or is it a trial?

Sometimes yes, sometimes a countdown. Free tiers usually limit one of three things: how many coins you can catalog, how many photos each entry holds, or valuation features that sit behind a paid plan. The trick is finding which limit applies before you have 500 rows in.

Questions to ask before entering 500 coins

Ask: is there a coin-count cap, and what happens when I hit it? Can I export everything, in a format like CSV or JSON, at any time? Does the free plan include the grade column I actually use, defined against a published standard such as the PCGS Grading Guide? And does the tool want a bank-style account, or does data stay on your phone?

Spreadsheets and local-first tools pass the export test. Where ours falls short: no automatic price lookups, so values are whatever you enter. If a free tier fails the export question, it is a trial wearing a free label.

How do I photograph and describe coins so my inventory is actually useful?

A photo is only useful if you can match it to a row in your spreadsheet years later. Two shots per coin is enough: obverse and reverse, on a neutral background (plain white paper works), under the same light every time. A window with indirect daylight beats a desk lamp because it avoids glare on mint luster. Shoot straight down, tap to focus, and include nothing else in the frame.

A repeatable photo setup

Same background, same distance, same light, every session. If you use a scale or ruler for size reference, keep it out of the obverse shot so you can crop cleanly later. Consistency matters more than quality; a mediocre photo taken the same way every time beats a beautiful one you cannot reproduce.

Naming photos so they match rows

Give every row an ID in your spreadsheet (a simple 001, 002 sequence works) and name each photo after it: 001-obverse.jpg, 001-reverse.jpg. Store photos in one folder alongside your spreadsheet or JSON backup, and the link survives moving to a new phone or computer. How do I organize my inventory by mint, year, grade, or storage location?

Storage location codes that scale

Give every physical spot a short code: S2-B3 means safe #2, box 3, slot position noted in a Notes column. Put the code in its own column, never inside the description, so sorting never breaks it. When you sell or move a coin, you edit one cell, not a paragraph. The point is answering "where is my 1916-D?" in under ten seconds.

Sorting and filtering recipes

Freeze the header row, then filter Mint = D, Year = 1916, and sort Grade descending. That single filter answers most questions a price check raises, such as what a key date is worth before you sell (PCGS Price Guide).

Honest limitation: a spreadsheet filters fast but shows one view at a time, and there is no offline mobile app baked into Excel to check a location code at a show. Our own tooling keeps records on your device with no account, but you still choose whether that fits better than a shared spreadsheet.

When should I move from a spreadsheet to dedicated inventory software?

Five triggers that mean the spreadsheet is losing

A spreadsheet works until it doesn't. Watch for these five triggers: you want a photo attached to every coin and the file is bloating past what your phone or laptop opens comfortably; you keep a separate document just to track what you paid versus what coins might be worth now; you're editing the file on a laptop but checking it at a coin show from your phone; the collection has grown past a few hundred coins and sorting by mint, year, and grade takes real discipline to keep consistent; or you've started duplicating the file into "backup v3 FINAL" because you no longer trust a single copy. Any one trigger is survivable. Two or more together mean the spreadsheet is losing.

How do I track coin values over time without paying for a subscription?

Log a value column with a date column, and update both yourself. Pull numbers from published price guides and recent auction results for the coins you actually care about, enter them with the date you checked, and your spreadsheet becomes a value history you own.

A quarterly value-update routine

Once a quarter, sort your sheet by value descending and reprice only the top slice, plus anything you are considering selling. Everything under a few dollars moves too slowly to matter. Add a note column entry for where the number came from, so future-you can judge whether it was a guide midpoint or an actual realized auction price.

What manual tracking cannot do

You will not get automatic daily repricing across thousands of lines, and you must accept that your numbers are snapshots, not a live feed. That is the honest trade: a subscription buys freshness you mostly do not need, while a dated manual log keeps your records on your own machine with no account anywhere.

Which free option fits which collector?

Under a few hundred coins, stay with the spreadsheet. It works offline, keeps records on your own phone or computer, and exports a backup you control. That is the honest ceiling for most collections, and it costs nothing.

Photo-heavy catalogs outgrow a spreadsheet fast. If you want one image per coin plus condition notes, dedicated software handles the file management you would otherwise fake with a folder of renamed JPEGs.

Paid tiers earn their price only when valuation automation matters, meaning you want values refreshed without typing them yourself. If you are comfortable checking prices manually and recording them in your sheet, keep the monthly fee in your pocket.

What is the best free way to keep records of my coins on my own phone without an account?

A spreadsheet on your phone or computer: one row per coin with columns for denomination, year, mint mark, grade, purchase price, storage location, and notes. Export a backup regularly so a lost device never costs you the catalog. No account and no server sits between you and your data.

How many coins can I catalog before a spreadsheet becomes unmanageable?

A few hundred is the practical ceiling for most collectors. Past that, sorting by mint, year, and grade takes real discipline, photo attachments bloat the file, and people start making backup copies because they no longer trust one file. Two triggers together mean dedicated software is the better fit.

Do I need to record the grade of every coin, or only the valuable ones?

Record a grade for every coin, even a rough one like circulated. Gaps in a first catalog are fine and can be filled in later, one session at a time, but an empty grade column breaks sorting and filtering later. Unless you have paid for grading, record it as your own opinion so the sheet stays honest.

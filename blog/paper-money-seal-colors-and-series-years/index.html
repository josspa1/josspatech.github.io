Seal color and series year are the first fields experienced collectors read on a U.S. note. This article maps the common seal colors to note types, explains what series year does and does not mean, and marks where hand lookup turns into a pile of open guides.

Seal colors and what they usually mark

Modern collectors still sort circulating and near-circulating U.S. notes by seal and obligation language. Typical associations:

  • Green seal — Federal Reserve Notes (the standard type in circulation today).
  • Blue seal — historically associated with silver certificates (redeemable in silver under older law; those notes are collectible now, not a current redemption path for silver).
  • Red seal — United States Notes (legal tender notes), distinct from Federal Reserve Notes.
  • Brown seal — specialty issues such as Hawaii overprint notes from World War II (and some other limited issues); treat brown as a flag to read the overprint and series carefully.

Always confirm with the obligation text and denomination design, not seal color alone. Counterfeits and fantasy notes exist; seal color is a sorting key, not authentication by itself.

What “series year” means on a note

The series year on a U.S. note identifies a signature combination and design series, not necessarily the calendar year the sheet was printed. A Series 2017A note, for example, belongs to that signature/design series; printing can continue after the calendar year in the label. When signatures change or a design revision is designated, the Bureau of Engraving and Printing advances the series letter or year per its rules.

Why series still matters for value

Collectors price many notes by denomination + series + district (for FRNs) + condition. Star notes (replacement notes) and certain districts carry different premiums. Getting the series wrong sends you to the wrong line in a guide.

Looking up value by hand

Identify type from seal color and face text. Read series year and signatures. Note the Federal Reserve bank letter/number on FRNs. Grade condition honestly (folds, soil, tears). Then open a paper-money price guide or auction comps for that exact type/series/denomination.

FAQ

Does a blue seal always mean a silver certificate?

Blue seals are strongly associated with silver certificates on classic U.S. notes, but you still confirm with the obligation wording and design. Do not price from seal color alone.

Is an older series year automatically worth more?

No. Common series in low grade often sell near face or a small premium. Scarcer combinations, stars, and high grade drive price more than “older” as a word.

Should I press folds out of a note before photographing it?

Do not iron or chemically flatten collectible notes; that can be considered damage. Photograph flat under even light; record folds in the condition notes.

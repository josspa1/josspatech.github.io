Pocket Watch Grades and Sizes Explained

American railroad-era pocket watches used a shared language of size and grade. This article defines those terms, shows where the marks live, and marks the step where flipping between catalogs for one movement becomes the bottleneck.

What “size” means on a pocket watch

Size is a traditional American movement measurement class, not the case diameter in millimeters you might expect from modern wristwatches. Common classes include 18-size and 16-size open-face or hunting movements; smaller sizes appear in thinner or ladies’ pieces. Case makers built cases to fit a stated size. A 16-size movement in an 18-size case (or the reverse) is a mismatch you can see once both are identified.

Where size is marked

Look on the movement plates and sometimes on the case dust cover or cuvette. Sellers often list “16s” or “18s” from those marks. If only the case is marked, treat that as a case claim until the movement confirms it.

What “grade” means (and what it does not)

Grade is a factory designation for a movement’s quality tier within a maker’s line — finish, adjustment claims, and often jewel count sit behind that name. Elgin, Waltham, Hamilton, Illinois, and other American makers published grade lists. A grade name is not a modern “condition grade” like a coin’s AU-50; it is a model/quality label from the factory catalog.

Grade vs. jewels vs. adjustments

Jewel count (for example 17 jewels, 21 jewels) and adjustment statements (to temperature, positions) often travel with grade, but they are separate facts. Two movements can share a jewel count and differ in grade finish. Record size, grade name, jewel count, and serial as four fields, not one combined string.

Open-face vs. hunting case

Movement layout and dial orientation differ between open-face and hunting-case watches. Size still describes the movement class; the case type is a separate attribute for fit and value.

How collectors decode markings by hand

Open the case carefully. Photograph the dial, movement, and any serial or grade stamps. Note maker, size mark, grade mark, jewel count if stated, and case metal marks (for example gold-filled stamps). Then match the grade name and serial range in a factory table or a compiled reference for that maker.

Why the lookup stalls

Makers reused similar names across eras. Serial ranges determine approximate production period. Case serials and movement serials can disagree when a movement was re-cased. Getting from “Hamilton 16-size, grade stamped X” to a dated production window and a market comparison means opening more than one table — often while standing at a show with the piece in hand.

FAQ

Is a higher grade always worth more?

Not by itself. Condition, completeness, case metal, and demand for that maker/grade matter. A worn high-grade movement in a mismatched case can sell for less than a clean mid-grade example in an original case.

Do European pocket watches use the same size system?

No. The American size classes are a U.S. trade convention. Swiss and other makers used different sizing and naming; do not force an American size label onto a movement that was never marked that way.

Can I identify grade from the dial alone?

Sometimes the dial names the maker; grade usually lives on the movement. A dial-only photo is a start. A movement photo is what grade decoding needs.
